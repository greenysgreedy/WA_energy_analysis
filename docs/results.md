# Grid battery revenue in the SWIS since the WEM reform

**Period:** trading days from 1 October 2023, the New WEM Commencement Day, to 1 November 2026.
**Scope:** 100 MW batteries at 1, 2 and 4 hours, merchant and holding Capacity Credits.
**Money:** nominal dollars for historical revenue; real 2025 dollars for the economics.

Every rule-derived or judgement-call assumption is recorded with its clause reference in
[`assumptions.md`](assumptions.md). Figures below are generated by the notebooks in
`notebooks/` and the tables in `outputs/`.

---

## 1. Headline

A 100 MW battery in the SWIS could have earned a great deal in the first full year after
the reform, and almost nothing by the third. The opportunity has closed faster than the
batteries chasing it were built.

**Perfect foresight, energy and FCESS only, over the whole period** (1,118 complete
trading days):

| Duration | Perfect foresight | Realisable | Capture rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 hour | $45.4m | $14.4m | 32% |
| 2 hours | $61.5m | $24.2m | 39% |
| 4 hours | $77.6m | $39.1m | 50% |

**By year**, as revenue per MW-year for the 2-hour merchant case:

| Year | Perfect foresight | Realisable | Capture rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024 | $364,000 | $163,000 | 45% |
| 2025 | $119,000 | $54,000 | 45% |
| 2026 to date | $48,000 | −$1,400 | negative |

Revenue per MW-year for the merchant cases, on the best naive forecast:

| Duration | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 to date |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 hour | $99,900 | $34,800 | −$2,500 |
| 2 hours | $163,300 | $53,800 | −$1,400 |
| 4 hours | $257,000 | $83,000 | $2,800 |

Three findings sit behind those numbers, and each is more useful than the totals.

**The arbitrage opportunity has closed.** The median daily settlement spread fell from
$577/MWh in 2024 to $145/MWh in 2025 and $91/MWh in 2026. The share of intervals priced
below zero fell from 25% to 5% to 1%. Contingency raise clearing prices fell from
$45/MWh on average in 2024 to $2.24/MWh in 2026.

**By 2026 a naive strategy loses money.** Perfect foresight still finds $4.7m a year for
a 2-hour battery, but every naive forecast tested returns a loss once the throughput cost
is charged. The spread is now narrow enough that acting on an imperfect signal costs more
than it earns, and the best naive policy is to do almost nothing.

**Capacity revenue, not trading revenue, is what makes a SWIS battery viable.** A 4-hour
battery holding Capacity Credits earns $164,000 to $212,000 per MW-year from the Reserve
Capacity Mechanism alone. On 2025 revenue that is nearly three times its trading income.
No merchant case in any year and at any duration reaches a positive NPV except the 4-hour
battery on 2024 revenue.

## 2. What the market settles, and why it matters

The single most consequential thing established in this project is a rule, not a number.

**Energy is settled on the thirty-minute Reference Trading Price, not the five-minute
Energy Clearing Price.** ESM Rules 9.9.4 gives the energy trading amount as
`ReferenceTradingPrice(t) × NetTradingQuantity(p,t)`, and clause 7.11A.1(b) defines that
price as the time-weighted average of the six five-minute clearing prices in the Trading
Interval. **FCESS is different**: clause 9.10.6 pays enablement per five-minute Dispatch
Interval at that interval's clearing price.

So a battery dispatches against five-minute constraints but is paid for energy on a
thirty-minute average. Any spread that exists only within a half hour is unavailable to
it however fast it is. Over 2024 the daily spread measured on five-minute prices was
$792/MWh at the median; measured on the price energy actually settles on it was
$577/MWh. Roughly a quarter of the apparent opportunity is an artefact of looking at the
wrong series.

The model therefore co-optimises at five minutes and settles energy at thirty. Notebook
03 reports what the alternative would have shown, so the size of the error is visible
rather than asserted.

Loss factors adjust the settled **quantity**, not the price (clause 9.5.2), and apply to
energy only, not to FCESS. Because the same factor scales charging and discharging, a
loss factor away from 1.0 scales net arbitrage revenue roughly linearly and does not
change the optimal dispatch pattern.

## 3. How much FCESS a new entrant can actually win

This is the largest modelling judgement in the project, and it moves the answer by an
order of magnitude.

Left to its accreditation alone, the optimiser holds 50 MW in each of four services in
every interval. For a 100 MW battery that is about a quarter of the entire SWIS
contingency raise requirement, held continuously.

The published market requirement is not a usable ceiling either: in 2025 the storage
fleet's contingency *raise* enablement averaged 110% of it, because at a clearing price
near zero the dispatch engine enables more than the minimum at no cost.

The central case instead gives the entrant the enablement per accredited MW that the
**operating** storage fleet actually achieved in each interval. That is an observation,
not an assumption about the merit order, and it falls as the fleet grows.

The three ceilings, for the 100 MW / 2-hour merchant case under perfect foresight on 2024:

| FCESS ceiling | Total | Energy | FCESS | Cycles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fleet utilisation (central) | $36.4m | $33.0m | $4.6m | 1,121 |
| published requirement | $36.1m | $33.0m | $4.2m | 1,120 |
| accreditation only (upper bound) | $97.1m | $26.6m | $71.4m | 906 |

The accreditation-only figure is what this project would have reported had the market
requirement been ignored: it overstates total revenue by a factor of 2.7 and FCESS
revenue by a factor of 15. It is shown here precisely so it is not mistaken for a result.

All three ceilings are reported side by side in notebook 03, so a reader can see how much
of the answer rests on this choice. It rests on it heavily.

**Storage could not access FCESS at all until July 2024.** No SWIS battery was enabled
for any frequency service in the first nine months after the reform, presumably because
accreditation took time. The model therefore credits a new entrant with no FCESS revenue
in that window, which understates it.

## 4. Realisable against theoretical

Perfect foresight solves each trading day knowing every price in advance. The rolling
horizon re-optimises every half hour on a 24-hour forecast, commits only the next half
hour, and is settled on actual prices. Parameters were designed on 2024 and frozen;
`strategies.frozen_for()` ignores the year it is given, so the no-tuning claim is enforced
in code rather than promised.

One qualification on the bound. Perfect foresight closes the state of charge at the end of
every trading day, which prevents it from earning revenue by draining a battery it never
refills. That constraint is not free: sensitivity M3-1 shows it costs 23% of 2025
perfect-foresight revenue. The bound is therefore a bound on *day-contained* dispatch, and
every capture rate below is a share of that, not of an unconstrained maximum.

Capture rates with the same-time-yesterday forecast:

| Case | 2024 (design) | 2025 (test) | 2026 (test) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 hour | 37% | 40% | −7% |
| 2 hours | 45% | 45% | −3% |
| 4 hours | 56% | 54% | +5% |

The design year and the first test year agree to within three percentage points at every
duration, which is the main evidence that the strategy is not fitted to one year's price
shape. Longer durations capture more because a longer battery is more forgiving of a
mistimed charge: it does not have to be right about *which* half hour is cheapest.

Persistence is reported but is not a usable strategy. A flat price forecast implies no
arbitrage opportunity at all, so the battery barely trades, capturing 1% to 31% depending
on year and duration. Its value is as a floor, not as a benchmark.

### The obligation is a cost under foresight and a benefit without it

A battery holding Capacity Credits may not charge inside the published obligation window
and must hold enough energy when it opens to discharge at its credited capacity
throughout. Under perfect foresight that costs about 4% of trading revenue, as expected.

Under every naive forecast it is worth **more** than it costs, by 2% to 69%. Being forced
to hold charge into the evening window is a better policy than what a bad forecast
chooses. The obligation substitutes a rule for a forecast, and the rule is better.

This is a real result rather than an artefact: it holds across all three forecasts and
all three durations, and it disappears exactly when foresight is perfect, which is what
one would expect if the mechanism is forecast error rather than something structural.

## 5. Validation against the real batteries

Seven of the nine registered SWIS storage facilities had operated by the end of the
period. Their revenue was rebuilt from first principles — SCADA metering, published
enablement schedules, clearing prices and loss factors — and set beside the model.

| Facility | Rating | Duration | From | Revenue | $/MW-year | Actual ÷ perfect foresight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| KWINANA_ESR1 | 101 MW | 2.0 h | Jan 2024 | $31.2m | $109,000 | 47% |
| COLLIE_ESR1 | 200 MW | 4.3 h | Oct 2024 | $26.6m | $64,000 | 43% |
| KWINANA_ESR2 | 226 MW | 4.2 h | Apr 2025 | $16.1m | $45,000 | 36% |
| COLLIE_BESS2 | 300 MW | 4.3 h | Aug 2025 | $13.3m | $35,000 | 41% |
| COLLIE_ESR4 | 252 MW | 4.2 h | Jan 2026 | −$0.8m | −$3,600 | −5% |
| COLLIE_ESR5 | 253 MW | 4.2 h | Jan 2026 | −$1.4m | −$6,700 | −10% |
| ALINTA_WGP_ESR1 | 101 MW | 2.1 h | Aug 2026 | −$0.1m | −$3,700 | −6% |

**No battery exceeded the perfect-foresight bound.** That is the first thing the model
had to survive, and it did.

**The four established batteries achieved 36% to 47% of the bound**, against the model's
realisable estimate of 45% to 56% for the equivalent years. The model is therefore
somewhat optimistic but in the right range: its realisable figure is a reasonable
estimate of what a competent operator achieves, not a fantasy. The residual gap of five
to fifteen percentage points is consistent with outages and network constraints, which
the model omits and real operators cannot.

**The three batteries commissioned in 2026 lost money on energy.** This independently
corroborates the model's finding that 2026 realisable revenue is negative. Two separate
pieces of evidence — a forward model and seven real operators — say the same thing about
the same year.

### Revenue mix, and why the first mover did so much better

KWINANA_ESR1 earned $109,000 per MW-year, two to three times any battery that followed,
and $24.8m of its $31.2m came from FCESS. It was the only accredited storage facility for
most of 2024 and took a service whose clearing prices then averaged $45/MWh. By Q4 2025
batteries held 84% of contingency and regulation volume and the price had fallen to about
$5/MWh. The first mover captured a scarcity rent that no subsequent entrant can.

### The gaps, diagnosed

Following the diagnosis order in the project's backcast procedure:

* **Unit and sign.** The SCADA energy column was confirmed empirically as MWh per
  five-minute interval, unanimously across all twenty facilities above 100 MW. Reading it
  as average MW would have scaled every figure by twelve.
* **Price series.** Energy is settled on the thirty-minute price, as established in
  section 2. Using the five-minute series would have overstated revenue.
* **Loss factors.** Applied per facility per financial year from the annual WEM Loss
  Factor Reports. They range from 0.956 to 1.064 across the fleet, so they move revenue
  by a few per cent, not more.
* **Partial operating months.** Each facility is restricted to its first full operating
  month onwards, so commissioning does not drag the per-MW figures down.
* **Obligation intervals.** Modelled explicitly; see section 4.
* **FCESS displacing energy.** Represented in the co-optimisation through the headroom
  constraints, though at the enablement quantities a new entrant can win, headroom is
  rarely binding.
* **Outages and network constraints.** Not modelled. This is the most likely remaining
  explanation for the residual gap between 39–48% actual and 45–56% modelled: a real
  battery is sometimes unavailable, and the model never is.

## 6. Reconciliation against published figures

Three independent checks, all against AEMO publications rather than against the model's
own inputs.

**Whole-market FCESS enablement cost.** Multiplying every facility's published enablement
by the clearing price and summing gives a figure that can be compared directly with
AEMO's published quarterly total. This is the strongest check available because it does
not depend on anything about storage.

| Quarter | Modelled | Published (AEMO QED Q4 2025, §4.5.2) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 2024 | $43.5m | $41.7m | +4% |
| Q4 2025 | $7.0m | $7.2m | −3% |

**Battery share of FCESS.** Storage's share of the modelled market cost was 81% in Q4
2025 against AEMO's published 84% share of contingency and regulation *volume* — close
agreement. For Q4 2024 the modelled cost share is 20% against a published volume share of
36%, and the difference is explained rather than excused: storage was concentrated in the
cheap services. It held 66% of regulation raise, which carried only 13% of market cost at
$24/MWh, but just 22% of contingency raise, which carried 48% of market cost at $40/MWh.
A share of volume is not a share of cost when the mix is that skewed. By Q4 2025 storage
held 79% to 89% across all four services and the two measures converge.

**Battery energy capacity.** AEMO's QED Q4 2025 states COLLIE_ESR1 as 200 MW/800 MWh and
KWINANA_ESR2 as 225 MW/900 MWh. The method used here — inverting Capacity Credits through
the ESR accreditation rule — gives 200 MW/853 MWh and 226 MW/959 MWh, within 7% on energy
and within 1% on power, from a completely independent route.

### A data defect found during reconciliation

The per-service costs would not reconcile until a transposition was identified: in both
`market-clearing-prices-csv` and `market-schedule-csv` the columns headed **Contingency
Raise** and **Contingency Lower** carry each other's data. The requirement dataset is
labelled correctly. The evidence is in assumption D9 and asserted by
`scripts/check_service_mapping.py`.

Because the two files are transposed consistently, price × quantity products are correct
and revenue totals are unaffected. What it changes is service attribution and, in the
dispatch model, which headroom constraint each service consumes. Had it gone unnoticed,
the model would have required injection headroom to sell a service that in fact needs
withdrawal headroom.

## 7. Price impact

### Spreads collapsed as storage connected

Operating storage in the SWIS went from 101 MW in January 2024 to 1,433 MW by August
2026. Over the 38 months observed, the monthly rank correlation between operating storage
capacity and every quantity a battery earns from is strongly negative:

| Measure | Spearman correlation with operating storage MW |
|---|---|
| 2-hour capturable spread | −0.85 |
| daily maximum less minimum | −0.85 |
| sum of FCESS clearing prices | −0.94 |
| share of intervals priced below zero | −0.76 |

Thirty-eight months of one market during rapid structural change does not support a
causal claim, and demand, fuel costs and the rest of the generation fleet all moved over
the same period. The correlations are reported because the magnitude is large and because
the direction is what theory predicts, not because the sample identifies an effect.

### The event study is inconclusive

Median capturable spread in the 90 days after each battery's first full operating month,
less the 90 days before, with the same calendar window a year earlier as a control:

| Facility | Change at commissioning | Control window | Difference in differences |
|---|---|---|---|
| KWINANA_ESR1 | −$108 | no data | — |
| COLLIE_ESR1 | −$156 | +$146 | −$302 |
| KWINANA_ESR2 | −$28 | +$66 | −$94 |
| COLLIE_BESS2 | +$17 | +$149 | −$132 |
| COLLIE_ESR4 and ESR5 | −$47 | −$117 | +$70 |
| ALINTA_WGP_ESR1 | +$5 | +$17 | −$12 |

Five of six differences are negative, but the control windows swing by $150 to $265
between facilities, two batteries commissioned in the same month and cannot be separated,
and the whole period trends downwards anyway. With seven events, overlapping windows and
a strong secular trend, this design cannot carry weight. The capacity-against-spread
relationship above is the better evidence, and it is still only suggestive.

### Would a new 100 MW battery be a price taker?

Estimated by regressing the change in settlement price between consecutive dispatch
intervals on the change in dispatched energy requirement, separately in each price band.
**This is an estimated elasticity, not an observed offer curve** (assumption D3).

| Price band | Slope, $/MWh per MW | t | R² | Price move per 100 MW |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| lowest decile (charging) | 0.106 | 15.7 | 0.010 | $10.58 |
| middle | 0.043 | 34.9 | 0.006 | $4.34 |
| highest decile (discharging) | 0.199 | 15.3 | 0.009 | $19.94 |

Every slope is positive, as it must be, and all are precisely estimated — but the R²
values are around 0.01. A five-minute price change is overwhelmingly driven by things
other than the five-minute quantity change. So the *central estimate* is well identified
while the *predictive* content is slight; the figures should be read as an average
relationship, not as a forecast of any particular interval.

### The haircut, and why it now matters

A battery that moves the price pays twice: it charges into a higher price and discharges
into a lower one. The combined erosion is $10.58 + $19.94 = **$30.52/MWh**, roughly
constant in absolute terms across the period. What changed is the spread it is measured
against:

| Year | Median capturable spread | Erosion | Haircut |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | $404/MWh | $30.52 | 8% |
| 2024 | $350/MWh | $30.52 | 9% |
| 2025 | $120/MWh | $30.52 | 25% |
| 2026 | $73/MWh | $30.52 | 42% |

**A 100 MW entrant was close to a price taker in 2024 and is not one now.** At a 9%
haircut the 2024 revenue estimates stand with a modest discount. At 42% the 2026 and
forward-looking estimates do not: a new battery entering today would erode a substantial
part of what little spread remains, and two batteries entering together would erode more.
Sensitivity case PI-1 carries the haircut through to the NPV.

This is the same finding as section 1 seen from the other side. The spread did not merely
shrink; it shrank until a single new entrant is large relative to it.

## 8. Economics

Real 2025 dollars, 7% real pre-tax WACC, 20-year life. Capital costs are CSIRO GenCost
2025-26 Appendix Tables B.4 and B.5, Current policies scenario: $778/kWh at one hour,
$525/kWh at two and $385/kWh at four, plus 10% for connection and development.
Degradation is driven by the modelled throughput, and augmentation is costed at the
battery-component price of the year it occurs.

Each row annualises one year's realisable revenue across the whole project life. That is
deliberately crude — nobody expects 2024 to repeat for twenty years — but it brackets the
answer better than a forecast would.

### With Capacity Credits

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 h | 2024 | $104,700 | $41,100 | $129,100 | +$16.8m | 9.4% |
| 1 h | 2025 | $36,300 | $53,100 | $121,500 | −$31.1m | 1.7% |
| 1 h | 2026 | −$1,900 | $45,600 | $119,400 | −$72.5m | −11.2% |
| 2 h | 2024 | $171,100 | $82,200 | $174,600 | +$75.5m | 14.7% |
| 2 h | 2025 | $55,000 | $106,100 | $164,800 | −$3.6m | 6.6% |
| 2 h | 2026 | $0 | $91,200 | $160,800 | −$66.9m | −2.3% |

Note that capacity revenue exceeds trading revenue for every duration on 2025 and 2026
revenue, and for the 4-hour battery it is close to doing so even in 2024. The Reserve
Capacity Price rose from $105,949 to $459,192 per MW-year across the period while the
energy spread collapsed, so the two moved in opposite directions.

### Merchant

Every merchant case is NPV-negative except the 4-hour battery on 2024 revenue, which
clears by $15.0m at an 8.2% IRR. On 2025 revenue the 2-hour merchant case is $107m under
water and the 4-hour case $149m. Without Capacity Credits, a SWIS battery built today does
not pay for itself on any recent year's trading revenue.

### The range, not a number

Across revenue years and a 5% to 10% real WACC, holding Capacity Credits:

| Duration | NPV range | Median NPV | IRR range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 hour | −$74.7m to +$35.1m | −$31.1m | −11% to +9% |
| 2 hours | −$75.9m to +$108.8m | −$3.6m | −2% to +15% |
| 4 hours | −$66.7m to +$242.4m | +$54.6m | +4% to +19% |

The spread across revenue years is far wider than the spread across any financial
assumption. That is the result: the answer depends almost entirely on which year's market
you assume, and the recent years are the bad ones. Only the 4-hour battery has a positive
median, and only it clears breakeven on 2025 revenue.

### The registered sensitivity cases

The six cases registered in `assumptions.md` are run by `scripts/run_sensitivities.py`.
Around the central case — 2 hours, Capacity Credits, 2025 revenue, NPV −$3.6m, IRR 6.6%:

| Case | What varies | NPV change |
|---|---|---|
| F-2 | entrant's FCESS share × 2.0 | +$23.4m |
| F-2 | entrant's FCESS share × 0.5 | −$11.7m |
| PI-1 | energy haircut of 25.5% from own price impact | −$9.3m |
| F-1 | FCESS performance factor 0.90 | −$2.3m |
| LF-1 | loss factor 0.95 / 1.05 | ∓$1.8m |

The FCESS share dominates, which is the same conclusion as section 3 reached from the
other direction, and it is the number a reviewer should attack first. The price-impact
haircut is next, and it is the case that most clearly turns a marginal project into a bad
one.

Two cases need a caveat rather than a number.

**M3-1, the terminal state of charge.** Freeing the daily closing state of charge raises
2025 perfect-foresight revenue by **23%**, from $11.9m to $14.6m. The bound reported
throughout this write-up is therefore a bound on *day-contained* dispatch, not on
dispatch in general. Real batteries can carry charge across the 08:00 trading-day
boundary, so the actual-against-bound ratios in section 5 are conservative by roughly
this amount — meaning the real batteries were capturing somewhat less of the true maximum
than the 36% to 47% figures suggest.

**D4-1, the duration range.** As implemented this pairs a duration's capital cost with
the nearest headline duration's revenue, so a 1.5-hour battery is charged 1.5-hour capex
against 1-hour revenue. The resulting NPVs of −$90m and −$43m overstate the penalty and
should be read as an upper bound on duration risk, not an estimate of it. Doing this
properly requires re-running the dispatch at the intermediate durations.

### What drives it

The tornado chart in notebook 07 ranks sensitivities around the 2-hour, 2025-revenue,
Capacity Credit case. Trading revenue and capacity revenue dominate; capital cost is
third; the WACC, fixed O&M, cycle life and project life are all second-order by
comparison. A reader who wants a different answer should change the revenue assumption,
not the discount rate.

**A caveat on the 1-hour case.** Under the modelled strategy it cycles 1,892 times in
2024, around five cycles a day, and the economics then charge it ten augmentations over
twenty years. A $5/MWh throughput cost is too low to restrain a battery facing 2024
spreads. The 1-hour figures should be read as optimistic on revenue and pessimistic on
degradation cost at the same time; the cycles-per-year sensitivity in the tornado chart
spans the plausible range. The 4-hour case does not have this problem: it cycles 632
times a year in 2024 and 506 in 2025, which is within normal operating practice.

## 9. Limitations

**No offer data.** Post-reform Real-Time Market Submissions are participant-only.
`effective-balancing-submission` stops at September 2023 and this project does not use
developer APIs. The price-impact analysis therefore estimates an elasticity rather than
reading a supply curve, and nothing in section 7 should be read as an observed offer.

**FCESS enablement is the scheduled quantity.** Clause 9.10.6 settles on the Essential
System Service Enablement Quantity published under 7.13.1E(c). What is public is the
dispatch run's schedule quantity, which may differ after outage adjustments. The
whole-market reconciliation in section 6 bounds the resulting error at a few per cent.

**Uplift is excluded.** Both Energy Uplift and FCESS Uplift require offer data to
compute. Excluding them makes modelled revenue a lower bound on those components. For
scale, FCESS Uplift was $50.9m across the whole market in Q4 2024 and $2.1m in Q4 2025
after the FCESS Cost Review.

**The entrant's FCESS share is calibrated, not derived.** The fleet-utilisation ceiling
assumes a new battery wins enablement at the same rate per accredited MW as the operating
fleet. A faster or better-sited battery would do better; a late entrant into a saturated
market would do worse.

**Performance factors are assumed to be 1.0.** They are not published per facility per
interval. This makes modelled FCESS revenue an upper bound on that component.

**Battery energy capacity is inferred.** No MWh figure is published. Capacity Credits
inverted through the accreditation rule agree with AEMO's stated figures to within 7%
where both exist, which is the best available evidence but not a measurement.

**Outages and network constraints are not modelled.** The model's battery is always
available. Real ones are not.

**One market, three years, nine batteries.** Every correlation in section 7 is drawn from
about 37 months of a single market during an unusually rapid structural change. The
sample does not support strong causal claims.

## 10. What a reviewer would challenge first

In the order a sceptical reader should attack this.

1. **The FCESS enablement ceiling.** It is worth an order of magnitude and it is a
   calibration, not a rule. If a new entrant could win enablement at twice the fleet rate,
   the 2025 revenue figures rise materially and some negative NPVs turn positive. This is
   the first number to stress.

2. **Annualising a single year across a twenty-year life.** No battery developer believes
   2024 or 2026 repeats forever. The honest reading is that the truth lies between the
   2025 and 2026 columns and that the Reserve Capacity Price — which rose from $105,949
   to $459,192 per MW-year across the period — matters more than the energy market does.

3. **Whether the naive forecasts are too naive.** Real operators use weather, demand and
   unit-commitment forecasts. The 39% to 48% that real batteries actually achieved sits
   just below the modelled 45% to 56%, which suggests the naive benchmark is about right
   in aggregate — but that comparison is confounded by outages, which the model omits and
   real operators cannot.

4. **The capacity-credit derating.** Accredited capacity is derived as deliverable energy
   spread over the four-hour obligation, net of discharge losses and the reserved
   state-of-charge window. That gives a 4-hour battery 94 MW of credits per 100 MW
   installed and a 1-hour battery 23 MW. AEMO's actual accreditation process is more
   involved, and since capacity revenue is now the dominant line, an error here is an
   error in the conclusion.

5. **The transposed contingency columns.** A reviewer should re-run
   `scripts/check_service_mapping.py` and satisfy themselves independently. If the
   relabelling is wrong, the per-service attribution in section 6 is wrong — though the
   totals would stand either way.

6. **The 2026 negative result.** It depends on a $5/MWh throughput cost being charged
   against a spread that has nearly closed. Checked directly in
   `scripts/check_2026_sign.py` for the 2-hour case over 2026 to date: realisable revenue
   is **+$0.06m at zero throughput cost, −$0.15m at $5/MWh and −$0.47m at $15/MWh**. So
   the sign is a throughput-cost artefact and should not be quoted as a finding. What is
   robust is the magnitude: every variant lands within half a million dollars of zero,
   against $16.3m for the same battery in 2024. There is almost nothing left to capture.

7. **Loss factor sourcing.** Parsed from PDF reports with a code-to-facility mapping that
   is a judgement call for the facilities without a dedicated Transmission Loss Factor
   code. The effect is a few per cent on energy revenue and nothing on FCESS.
